Chapter 4 · Calculus Done Properly

What We Have Learned

We began by asking what an integral really is, what integrals can prove, and what to do when an integral has no formula. Here is how the chapter’s nine questions answered them.

1. What is an integral, and which functions have one?

In Section 4.1 we defined the integral as the common limit of lower and upper sums of thin rectangles, and computed \(\int_0^a x^5\,\mathrm{d}x\) from that definition alone. It took some work: telescoping sums of consecutive products, bounds on both sides, and a squeeze. That effort is the measure of how much the fundamental theorem of calculus gives you for free.

The definition also told us that not every function is integrable. Dirichlet’s function, \(1\) on the rationals and \(0\) on the irrationals, has upper sums all equal to \(1\) and lower sums all equal to \(0\). Problems like this one led Lebesgue to a more powerful theory of integration, which you will meet later at university.

2. What can integrals prove?

Far more than areas.

  • Inequalities between numbers. An integral of a positive function equals \(\frac{22}{7} - \pi\), so \(\frac{22}{7} > \pi\) (Section 4.3). The idea, “write \(A - B\) as the integral of something positive”, is as useful as it is simple.

  • Infinite products and special values. Reduction formulae for \(\int \sin^n t\,\mathrm{d}t\), an invariant and a squeeze gave Wallis’s product, the constant in Stirling’s formula and \(\left(\frac12\right)! = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\) (Section 4.4).

  • Infinite sums. Expanding \(x^x\) as a power series and integrating term by term gave the sophomore’s dream (Section 4.5).

  • The foundations of trigonometry. Defining \(\mathrm{T}(x) = \int_0^x \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{1+u^2}\) and \(\pi = 2\mathrm{T}(\infty)\), substitutions alone produced the addition formula and special values like \(\mathrm{T}(\sqrt3) = \frac\pi3\) (Section 4.2).

  • Geometry. An integrand that simplified to a constant revealed Archimedes’ hat-box theorem (Section 4.8), and an area formula written as an integral round the boundary proved Holditch’s astonishing theorem (Section 4.9).

  • Optimal functions. Completing the square inside an integral showed exactly when \(\int\bigl((y')^2 - a^2y^2\bigr)\) must be non-negative, and the critical value \(\frac\pi2\) turned out to be an eigenvalue (Section 4.7).

3. What can we do when there is no formula?

In Section 4.6 we proved that \(\int \frac{e^x}{x+1}\,\mathrm{d}x\) cannot be written as a rational function times \(e^x\), and Liouville’s theory shows it has no elementary formula at all. The reason, seen through the lens of poles, is that the antiderivative must behave like a logarithm near \(x = -1\), which rational functions cannot do. Yet the integral exists perfectly well, and we can compute it: numerically, by series (as for \(\int x^x\) in Section 4.5), or by giving it a name and studying it as a new function, as we did with the W function in Section 3.9.

If you want to go further before university

  • Paul Nahin, Inside Interesting Integrals (Springer): a feast of clever integrals and the tricks behind them.

  • William Dunham, The Calculus Gallery: masterpieces of analysis from Newton to Lebesgue.

  • Michael Spivak, Calculus: the definition of the integral and the fundamental theorem, done properly.

  • Sherman Stein, Archimedes: for the ancient roots of integration.

Hermite recoiled from functions without derivatives, but integration has never had that problem: it is generous, accepting functions that differentiation cannot handle, and it keeps producing surprises. If a single theme runs through this chapter, it is that integrals are not just a technique for finding areas. They are a language for expressing and proving facts about numbers, functions and shapes, and you will be speaking it for the rest of your mathematical life.
