Chapter 6 \(\cdot\) Introduction

From Equations to Structures

Mathematics is the art of giving the same name to different things.

— Henri Poincaré

For most of its history, algebra meant solving equations. The Babylonians solved quadratics four thousand years ago; Italian mathematicians of the sixteenth century solved cubics and quartics; and then, for nearly three hundred years, the quintic resisted every attempt. The resolution, when it came in the 1820s and 1830s, changed algebra completely. Niels Henrik Abel proved that no general formula for the quintic exists, and Évariste Galois explained exactly which equations can be solved, by studying not the equations themselves but the symmetries of their roots. To do so he had to invent a new kind of mathematical object: the group.

That shift, from computing answers to studying structures, is the subject of this chapter. Instead of asking “what is the solution?”, modern algebra asks “what kind of object is this, and what operations does it support?” The answers turn out to explain why the old tricks worked, and to settle questions that tricks alone never could.

Why structure matters

Here are three ancient problems that resisted all attempts for two thousand years: with ruler and compasses, double the volume of a cube; trisect an arbitrary angle; construct a regular \(7\)-sided polygon. None of them can be done, and the proofs, found in the nineteenth century, are not clever constructions but arguments about structure: about the degree of the field of numbers that ruler and compasses can reach (Section 6.3). The same theory explains why the regular \(17\)-gon can be constructed (Section 6.2), and why the quartic can be solved but the quintic cannot (Section 6.4).

Structures also travel. The axioms for a group are satisfied by the symmetries of a crystal, the permutations of the roots of a polynomial, the rotations of three-dimensional space, the moves of Rubik’s cube and the points of an elliptic curve. A theorem proved once from the axioms holds in all of them. That economy, giving “the same name to different things” as Poincaré put it, is what makes abstract algebra so powerful.

What you will meet at university

In the first year you will meet groups (sets with an operation satisfying three axioms) and, usually, rings and fields (sets with addition and multiplication). You will prove Lagrange’s theorem, that the size of a subgroup divides the size of the group, and meet permutations, symmetry groups and matrix groups. In the second and third years these lead to Galois theory, which connects fields and groups and answers the classical questions about equations and constructions; to representation theory, which studies groups through matrices; and to algebraic number theory, where the ideas of Section 1.7 are put on a firm footing. The last three sections of this chapter are about groups. Groups are no longer on the STEP syllabus (they were removed in 2019), but the questions are self-contained and each section gives the definitions it needs.

The questions that run through this chapter

Three big questions

  1. What can be built from what? Which numbers can be reached from the rationals by square roots, or by solving equations, and how do we prove something cannot be reached?

  2. Why do the formulas for equations work? What is the hidden reason that quadratics, cubics and quartics have formulas, and what changes at degree five?

  3. What is gained by abstraction? What happens when we describe objects only by the rules they obey?

How to read this chapter

Many of these questions can be done by brute force: expand everything, compare coefficients, check every case. Do that first if you need to. Then look back and ask what made the brute force work, and whether there is a structural reason behind it. The structural reason is usually shorter, always more illuminating, and it is what you will need when the brute force becomes impossible.
