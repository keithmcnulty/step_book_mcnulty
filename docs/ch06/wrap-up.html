Chapter 6 · Algebra and Structure

What We Have Learned

We began with the shift in algebra from solving equations to studying structures, and with three questions: what can be built from what, why the formulas for equations work, and what is gained by abstraction. Here is what the chapter’s eight questions showed.

1. What can be built from what?

In Section 6.3 we eliminated radicals one at a time to find polynomials satisfied by \(\sqrt2 + \sqrt3\), \(\sqrt2 + \sqrt3 + \sqrt5\) and \(\sqrt[3]2 + \sqrt[3]3\), of degrees \(4\), \(8\) and \(9\). Those degrees are invariants: the degree of the field a number generates. Because degrees multiply up a tower of fields, numbers reachable by square roots have degrees that are powers of \(2\). That single fact proves that the cube cannot be doubled (\(\sqrt[3]2\) has degree \(3\)) and that a \(60^\circ\) angle cannot be trisected.

In the other direction, Section 6.2 showed how something can be built. Splitting the \(17\)th roots of unity into Gauss’s periods, the sum \(\cos\frac{2\pi}{17} + \cos\frac{4\pi}{17} + \cos\frac{8\pi}{17} + \cos\frac{16\pi}{17}\) satisfied a quadratic, with root \(\frac{-1 + \sqrt{17}}{4}\); three more quadratics lead to \(\cos\frac{2\pi}{17}\), and so to the construction of the regular \(17\)-gon. Underneath both sections were the cyclotomic polynomials of Section 6.1, the building blocks of \(x^n - 1\), obtained by sorting the roots of unity by their order.

2. Why do the formulas for equations work?

Section 6.4 showed the secret of the quartic: from four roots, form the three quantities \(\alpha\beta + \gamma\delta\), \(\alpha\gamma + \beta\delta\), \(\alpha\delta + \beta\gamma\), one for each way of pairing the roots. Any rearrangement of the roots merely shuffles these three, so they satisfy a cubic whose coefficients are known. Solving the cubic, then two quadratics, gave the roots. Lagrange saw that this works because of how permutations of four objects act on the three pairings; Galois saw that it fails for five roots because the group of permutations of five objects contains a piece that cannot be broken down. The formulas are shadows of group structure.

3. What is gained by abstraction?

The last four sections described objects by the rules they obey.

  • In Section 6.5, four rules (additivity, the product rule, constants, \(\lozenge x = 1\)) forced an operation to be differentiation, with no limits in sight. The same rules power automatic differentiation in modern software.

  • In Section 6.6, a three-line argument from the group axioms showed that no group is the union of two proper subgroups, for every group at once.

  • In Section 6.7, a strange operation on pairs of complex numbers turned out to be Hamilton’s quaternion multiplication, and representing it by matrices made associativity automatic.

  • In Section 6.8, reading the definition of a group literally produced a group of singular matrices whose identity is not the identity matrix, and which is “really” the group of non-zero real numbers in disguise.

In each case, the abstract view explained something that calculation alone would have obscured.

If you want to go further before university

  • Mario Livio, The Equation That Couldn’t Be Solved: the quintic, Galois and the birth of group theory.

  • Nathan Carter, Visual Group Theory: groups through pictures, the ideal first look.

  • M. A. Armstrong, Groups and Symmetry: a first university course, built on geometry.

  • Ian Stewart, Galois Theory: for when you are ready for the full story.

Galois died at twenty, after a duel, leaving a few dozen pages that took the mathematical world decades to understand. What he saw was that an equation’s deepest secrets are not in its coefficients but in the symmetries of its solutions. That insight, giving the same name to different things and studying the structure they share, now runs through all of mathematics. You have seen it at work in polynomials, polygons, quaternions and matrices; at university you will see it almost everywhere.
